I make movies. I write screenplays, direct, edit, and occasionally compose music. I also build production AI systems at a dental implant company. People keep treating those two as separate. They aren't.

<blockquote class="pull-quote">
The customer is the cast. The system is the scene. The job is to make the scene work for them.
</blockquote>

I'm cutting *Among Monsters* between work weeks right now.

## A film set looks like an engineering room

A small short has a director, a writer, a DP, a sound mixer, a gaffer, a costumer, an art department and an editor. Each one speaks a different vocabulary and defines "done" differently. The director's job is to translate the scene into the eight languages on set.

It's the same room I walk into at work. A treatment coordinator who has quoted full arch implant cases for years doesn't care how I store a frozen price. They care whether the system gives them the same number they quoted last month. The controller doesn't care about my retry decorator. They care whether the bank file lands in the cash flow sheet before the cutoff.

Specialists. None of them wrong. One person whose job is to make it add up.

## Things I do at a keyboard that I learned on set

<div class="aside aside-amber">

**Block before you light.** On set you walk the actors through the scene before anyone touches a lamp. In code I write the decision record before I touch the schema.

</div>

<div class="aside">

**Listen to the <span class="egg" tabindex="0" data-egg="On set the script supervisor notices the coffee cup moved between takes. In code that person writes the regression test.">script supervisor</span>.** Lowest credit on the call sheet, highest practical authority on the day. At work that's the person who does the job every day. Someone on the accounting team told me the bank import was grabbing half downloaded files, and that is why the import now stops and asks.

</div>

<div class="aside aside-emerald">

**Cut to the bone.** Editing a film is mostly removing, and the cuts that hurt are usually the right ones. Code is the same. Less is harder than more.

</div>

<div class="aside aside-amber">

**Show one cut to the right person.** My first rough cut goes to someone from the audience. I don't ask for notes. I watch them watch it. The first demo of a feature goes to the person who will use it every day, and I would rather watch them fumble through the tooth chart in silence than read a thread about it.

</div>

<div class="aside">

**A scene works or it doesn't.** Films get judged on whether the scene works. A polished sequence of bad scenes is a polished bad film. I'm relentless about whether the shipped thing solved the problem the customer brought.

</div>

## What this does to the engineering portfolio

**The systems I ship survive contact with non engineers.** The odd rules in the dashboard, the estimator and the consult pipeline came out of arguments with the people who use them.

**I can work alone in scopes that would normally need a team.** A director on a small short is also the writer, the editor, the colourist, the producer's bad cop and the actor's good cop. I came to engineering already used to wearing eight hats.

**The postmortem is part of the work.** I rewatch every film a year later with a list of what didn't work and why. My systems keep a backlog of deferred items with the trigger to revisit each one. Same instinct: name the loose ends out loud.

## The version a hiring manager wants

If you're hiring an engineer who can sit with a customer until the system you ship is the system they needed, that's the work I want. Treat every project like a small film: a spec before the schema, department heads who all get heard, a scene that has to work, and a cut that gets shorter every week until it's the right length.

Two crafts, same muscle.
